## At some point, being able to explain yourself stops being impressive.

I learned that later than I would have liked.

A man can know all the right words. Accountability. Attachment. Boundaries. Trauma. Repair. He can explain exactly why he behaves the way he does and still be exhausting to love.

Insight helps. It just does not get the final vote.

Grown Men Grow lives in the distance between understanding yourself and actually becoming easier to trust.

Most of us inherited some useful ideas about being men. Keep your word. Learn how to do hard things. Protect the people you love. Be someone others can count on when the situation gets ugly.

Good lessons.

We also inherited some weird shit.

Do not need too much. Do not look unsure. If something hurts, make it funny before anyone notices. If you cannot fix the problem, control the room. If you feel powerless, get angry. At least anger still feels like power.

Some of that may have helped us survive. That does not mean it should run the rest of our lives.

This is where adulthood becomes unfair.

We are not responsible for everything that happened to us. We are responsible for what happens through us once we know better.

That distinction can feel brutal. It is also the beginning of freedom.

Men’s pain is real. Some of it is fucking enormous.

Divorce. Rejection. Loneliness. Losing work that gave life shape. Watching a parent disappear by degrees. Not seeing your children enough. Waking up inside a body that no longer behaves like the one you remember. Realizing that the future you quietly counted on is not coming.

Men deserve language for that. We deserve friendship sturdy enough to hear it. We deserve something better than being told to toughen up or pay someone by the hour because nobody else wants the conversation.

But pain is not permission.

It does not give us the right to become cruel, controlling, sexually entitled, emotionally absent, or permanently exempt from the effects we have on other people. Hurt can explain behavior. It cannot keep doing the apologizing for us.

The same goes for shame.

A man saying “I’m a piece of shit” may sound accountable, but sometimes it is just another way to make the room revolve around him. Everyone else has to stop dealing with what happened and start convincing him he is still worth saving.

I know that move. It feels like remorse. It can also be an escape hatch.

Actual accountability is less dramatic. It asks what happened, what it cost, what needs repair, and what must change so the apology does not become a seasonal tradition.

That is the territory here.

We will talk about desire without pretending desire is dirty. We will also talk about the fact that wanting someone does not create a debt.

We will talk about anger without treating anger as proof of strength or evidence of moral failure. Anger carries information. It is still a terrible chief executive.

We will talk about honesty, including the kind that does not arrive with a knife in its hand.

We will talk about grief, sex, friendship, divorce, confidence, loneliness, fatherhood, ambition, failure, repair, and the unnerving experience of realizing that the version of you who got this far may not be the version who can take you any further.

I am not a therapist. I am not qualified to diagnose strangers, and I have no interest in becoming another man online who discovered six concepts and declared himself a guide.

I have done a lot of reading. I have spent years examining systems and human behavior professionally. I have also gotten important things wrong in my own life.

This publication comes from all three.

The personal history matters, but it will not become a public courtroom. Other people do not lose their privacy because I have decided to write. I will use my life when it helps illuminate the work. I will leave it alone when disclosure would serve only curiosity, vindication, or spectacle.

Grown Men Grow is written for men, but it is not only for men.

It is also for the people who love us, raise us, work beside us, sleep next to us, forgive us, leave us, and occasionally wonder whether we plan to take responsibility for our own interior lives.

Each week, I will publish one field note.

No ten-step system. No promise of reinvention by Tuesday. Just an honest attempt to look at one difficult thing without bullshitting ourselves about it.

The first field note is called:

[**Strength Has to Grow Up**](/strength-has-to-grow-up/)

It begins with a question I wish more of us encountered before life found a harsher way to ask it:

**What happens when the version of strength that protected you becomes the thing preventing you from growing?**
