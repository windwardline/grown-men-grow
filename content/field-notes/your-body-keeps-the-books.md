# Ghost essay source

There's a bookkeeping style that works right up until it doesn't: don't open the mail.

Men run their bodies this way for decades. The statements arrive, the knee that reports after basketball, the 3 a.m. ceiling-staring, the shirt that fits differently, the flight of stairs with a new opinion, and the statements go on the pile, unopened. Not because he can't read. Because he's afraid of the balance, and somewhere he picked up the idea that ignoring a number changes it.

The body doesn't mind. It keeps the books either way. It has never once lost a receipt.

## Every entry posts

That's the part worth respecting, even admiring: the accounting is flawless. The missed sleep posts. The desk decade posts. The stress that got "handled" by not mentioning it posts, with interest. So do the deposits: every walk, every vegetable that wasn't beige, every night that ended before midnight. Nothing's wasted and nothing's forgotten.

Men love systems like this everywhere else. Fair, precise, incorruptible. The only reason this one feels hostile is that we've been running a deficit and calling it toughness.

## Toughness got misfiled

Somewhere the file got corrupted, and ignoring the body got classified as strength. Playing hurt, sleeping four hours, treating the check-engine light as a personality test: badges, all of them, right up until the audit.

Actual toughness is filed the other way. It takes nerve to step on a scale you've been avoiding. It takes more to sit in a paper gown and hear numbers, or to say out loud to a doctor the sentence you've been editing for two years: something's off. The man who books the physical isn't fragile. He's the one reading his own mail.

The audit comes either way. The only choice a man gets is the auditor: the scheduled kind, in a clinic, on a Tuesday you picked, or the unscheduled kind, which picks its own date and has never once picked well.

## Reading the books

Reading your books isn't a wellness lifestyle. It's four unglamorous habits with decades of compounding behind them.

Open the mail. When the body reports, pain, sleep, appetite, mood, the stairs, log it as data instead of filing it under getting old. Getting old is real. It's also the most popular place men hide numbers they don't want to read.

Get audited on schedule: the physical, the dentist, the screening your decade calls for, booked by the calendar and not by crisis. You know this argument by now: by interval, not by feeling.

Make boring deposits. Sleep, food that grew somewhere, the walk, the weights. Deposits are small and the interest is quiet, which is exactly what men who love compound interest keep forgetting applies here.

Tell one person the real balance. A doctor, sure. But also one man who knows your actual numbers: the knee, the sleep, the weight of the thing you're carrying. Books only one person can read get cooked.

## The estate you're building

Here's the frame that finally lands with most men: you're not maintaining a body. You're funding the version of you who shows up later, the one at your kid's wedding, on the floor with a grandchild, carrying his own bag down some trail at seventy-something.

That man gets built now, out of entries you're posting this week. He gets whatever you deposit, plus interest, and pays for whatever you skipped, plus the same.

Open the mail. It's addressed to him.
