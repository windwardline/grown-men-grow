# Ghost essay source

There is a bookkeeping style that works right up until it doesn't: don't open the mail.

Men run their bodies this way for decades. The statements arrive — the knee that reports after basketball, the 3 a.m. ceiling-staring, the shirt that fits differently, the flight of stairs with a new opinion — and the statements go on the pile, unopened. Not because he can't read. Because he is afraid of the balance, and somewhere he learned that ignoring a number changes it.

The body doesn't mind. It keeps the books either way. It has never once lost a receipt.

## Every entry posts

That is the part worth respecting, even admiring: the accounting is flawless. The missed sleep posts. The desk decade posts. The stress that got "handled" by not mentioning it posts, with interest. So do the deposits — every walk, every vegetable that wasn't beige, every night that ended before midnight. Nothing is wasted and nothing is forgotten.

Men like systems like this in every other domain. Fair, precise, incorruptible. The only reason this one feels hostile is that we've been running a deficit and calling it toughness.

## Toughness got misfiled

Somewhere the file got corrupted: ignoring the body got classified as strength. Playing hurt, sleeping four hours, treating the check engine light as a personality test — badges, all of them, right up until the audit.

Actual toughness is the opposite filing. It takes nerve to step on a scale you've been avoiding. It takes more to sit in a paper gown and hear numbers, or say out loud to a doctor the sentence you've been editing for two years: something is off. The man who books the physical is not fragile. He is the one reading his own mail.

The audit comes either way. The only choice a man gets is the auditor: the scheduled kind, in a clinic, on a Tuesday you chose — or the unscheduled kind, which picks its own date and has never once picked well.

## Reading the books

Reading your books is not a wellness lifestyle. It is four unglamorous habits with decades of compounding behind them.

Open the mail: when the body reports — pain, sleep, appetite, mood, the stairs — log it as data instead of filing it under getting old. Getting old is real. It is also the most popular place men hide numbers they don't want to read.

Get audited on schedule: the physical, the dentist, the screening your decade demands, booked by the calendar and not by crisis. You know this argument by now: by interval, not by feeling.

Make boring deposits: sleep, food that grew somewhere, the walk, the weights. Deposits are small and the interest is quiet — which is exactly what men who love compound interest keep forgetting applies here.

Tell one person the real balance: a doctor, sure. But also one man who knows your actual numbers — the knee, the sleep, the weight of the thing you're carrying. Books that only one person can read get cooked.

## The estate you're building

Here is the frame that finally lands with most men: you are not maintaining a body. You are funding the version of you that shows up later — the one at your kid's wedding, on the floor with a grandchild, carrying his own bag down some trail at seventy-something.

That man is built now, out of entries you're posting this week. He gets whatever you deposit, plus interest — and pays whatever you skipped, plus the same.

Open the mail. It's addressed to him.
