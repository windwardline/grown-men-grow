# Ghost essay source

There's a contractor who only works nights. He doesn't advertise. He doesn't need to; he already has the address. Somewhere around ten to four he lets himself in, sits on the end of the bed, and starts pricing the house.

He's fast. He does the roof, the retirement account, the lump that's been there since August, the two-line email from the boss that said "Got a minute tomorrow?", the tone of a kid's voice at dinner, and the noise the truck made on the highway Thursday, all in about forty minutes. He's thorough. Nothing gets left off. And every job comes in at roughly the price of the house.

He's never once come in under. In fairness, he's never been inside any of the buildings.

## Every bid has a blind line

Anybody who bids work for a living learns early not to price a job over the phone. The customer says the bathroom floor is "a little soft by the tub," and that sentence covers everything from a loose tile to a joist that's been drinking from the drain line since the wallpaper was in fashion. So the estimator goes and looks. He opens the vanity, gets a flashlight into the crawlspace, pushes a screwdriver into the subfloor and watches how far it goes without being asked.

Whatever he can't see still has to go in the bid, and it goes in as contingency: money held against the part of the job nobody's looked at yet. The less he saw, the bigger that line. A bid with a proper walk carries a little. A bid off the phone carries a lot. A bid made with no walk at all, from bed, in the dark, is mostly contingency with a few real numbers on top for decoration.

That's the night guy's whole method. He doesn't lie, exactly. He just won't walk anything, and he prices every blind spot at the worst thing it could be hiding. The email isn't a meeting, it's a firing. The lump isn't a lump. The quiet kid is a ruined adulthood with a twenty-year lead time. It all adds up, because he's the one adding, and the total's always the same: everything.

In the morning the same jobs can be walked. Not because things look better in daylight. Sometimes they look exactly as bad, just better lit. The walk changes what kind of number it is.

The email gets answered, and a fair amount of the time it's about the holiday schedule. The lump gets a phone call, which costs a morning off and a paper gown nobody has ever looked good in, and whatever comes back comes back as a measurement instead of a rumour. The kid gets asked something sideways in the truck, which is where kids keep the real answers. The account gets opened, which is unpleasant in the specific way looking at a number is unpleasant, and still less unpleasant than the number the night guy made up.

The good estimators don't walk the big jobs alone, either. They bring somebody, because two flashlights find what one misses, and because a second man in the crawlspace who looks at the sill and says "yeah, that's bad, and here's how bad" is worth more than any amount of reassurance from the driveway. Most men have somebody who'd come and stand in the crawlspace. Plenty have done it for someone else on a Saturday without being asked twice.

## Sometimes he's right

Here's the part the morning doesn't fix. Every contractor has pulled up a floor and found it worse than he feared: the rot three feet further along than the screwdriver said, a leak that's been feeding the wall for a decade. The night guy is wrong about most of the jobs and right about some, and the ones he's right about don't shrink because somebody walked them.

What the walk buys isn't a better number. It's a job instead of a dread. A job has a start date. It has an order of operations, a first thing and a second thing, people whose trade it is, and a price that can usually be paid in pieces. A dread has none of that. It sits on the end of the bed, priced at everything, and waits for tomorrow night.

And for some men the night guy never clocks out. He's there every night, the daylight walk doesn't move a single figure, and the whole house is still priced at everything at noon. That isn't an estimating problem, and it'd be a lie to file it as one. It's a trade of its own, with people who do it for a living, and walking that one with a doctor is about as unusual as walking a roof with a roofer.

## Nothing gets signed at four

He'll be back tonight. He always is, with the clipboard and the flashlight he never turns on, and he'll have the place priced by half past. There's no firing him. He came with the house.

He just doesn't get to sign anything. That's the one rule in the trade that matters here: nothing gets signed off a bid nobody walked. Whatever he wrote down at four will still be there at seven, and at seven it can be walked, alone or with somebody holding the other flashlight. Which job gets walked first, and whether anybody comes along, is up to him.
