# Ghost essay source

Every backyard party has a man who hasn't sat down since noon. He's at the grill. He was at the grill when the first car pulled up, and he'll be at the grill when the last one leaves, scraping the grate with the focus of a surgeon closing.

He is, to be clear, doing a real job. People are going to eat. Somebody has to keep the chicken from becoming a cautionary tale, and burgers come off when they come off, not when a conversation reaches a natural pause. Nobody who's eaten a hockey puck at somebody else's cookout takes the grill lightly.

But it's worth noticing what else the grill does for him, because it does a lot.

## The best spot in the yard

The grill is a post. It gives him a place to stand, which is half of what makes a party hard. It gives his hands something to hold. It gives him a topic with every man who wanders over: the charcoal, the heat, whether gas is cheating. (It isn't. It is a little.) These conversations are pleasant, they last two minutes, and they're about the meat.

And it gives him an exit from anything. Any conversation that starts to go somewhere, he can leave with the five most respected words in the backyard: "I gotta flip these." Nobody's ever offended by that. Nobody follows him over, either, because nobody wants to stand in the smoke.

It even comes with a clock. A burger wants four or five minutes a side, which happens to be about as long as most men can talk to their wife's cousin about his solar panels. When the clock runs out, so does the conversation, and nobody's to blame. The meat said so.

So he's at the party the way the speakers are at the party. Essential, and not really in it.

From the lawn it looks like generosity, and it is generosity. That's what makes it such good cover. A man who spent the whole afternoon in the garage would get asked about it. A man behind a platter of perfectly charred bratwurst gets thanked.

## Who came, and for what

Here's the uncomfortable part.

Most of the people in that yard didn't come for the food. The food's good. The food is also available at any grocery store in the county. They came for the people, and he's one of the people. His brother drives ninety minutes for this. His oldest friend is in a lawn chair twenty feet away, telling the canoe story to somebody's wife, and the canoe story is better when he's there to deny it. His kid's birthday happens in this yard every year, and a fair number of the photographs of him from those afternoons are of his back, through smoke.

None of that's a crime. It's a trade, and it's worth looking at the price. Every hour at the post is an hour nobody asks him how he's doing, which is sometimes the whole appeal. The grill answers that question for him. He's doing great. Look at these ribs.

And somebody's noticed. Somebody who loves him clocked the post years ago, and at some point stopped mentioning it, which isn't the same thing as not minding. The people who want him in the circle aren't fooled by the platter. They've just decided it's not worth the argument at a party.

## The tongs fit anybody

The obvious move is to hand them over, and the obvious move costs something real, so there's no point pretending it doesn't.

The burgers will be worse. That's just true. Whoever takes the tongs is going to press the patties flat with the spatula to hear them hiss, which squeezes the juice into the coals and is, among people who grill, roughly a felony. He'll have to watch this from a lawn chair. He may have to sit on his hands.

And then, sitting down, he's available. Somebody asks how work is going, and means it. Somebody asks about his dad. The grill was never only shelter from small talk. It was shelter from the big kind.

There's also a man for whom the post isn't a hiding place at all. It's the only way through the gate. The party's at his ex's sister's house, or it's the first one since the funeral, or he's never been easy in a crowd and the grill is how he gets to come at all. For him the tongs aren't a dodge. They're a ticket, and nobody should take a man's ticket to make a point about being present. Some afternoons, standing in the smoke with a job is exactly the right amount of party.

But a lot of afternoons, for a lot of the men who've held the tongs since noon, it isn't about getting through. It's habit, and a little of it is that being useful is easier than being company. The job's real. It's also a job anyone with two hands and a decent attention span can do, which is the most annoying thing about it.

Somewhere in the yard there's a brother-in-law who'd love to be asked. He'll press the burgers flat. Everyone will eat them anyway. And for the length of one batch, the man who's been at the grill since noon can sit in a folding chair with a paper plate on his knee, close enough to smell what he'd have done differently, and hear the canoe story told with him in it.
