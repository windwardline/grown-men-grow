# Ghost essay source

Anybody who's put a spare on in a driveway knows the moment. The wheel's up on the studs, the first nut's spinning on, and there's a strong, almost moral urge to run that one all the way down before touching the others. It's right there. It's going on. Why leave a job half done?

Because a wheel isn't five jobs. It's one.

Any shop manual gives the order, and so will the guy at the tire place if you catch him before he picks up the gun. Start every nut by hand, so none of them goes on crooked. Snug them in a star: across the wheel, skip one, across again, until they're all just touching. Let the car down onto the tire. Then tighten them in the same star, in passes, a bit at a time, so the wheel comes down flat on the hub from every side at once.

Run one nut all the way first and it pulls the wheel toward that stud. The wheel can seat a hair crooked, hard against the hub on one side and barely kissing it on the other, and nothing about it looks wrong. The car drives home. The car drives fine for a month.

Then one day, braking for an exit, the pedal starts to pulse under his foot and the steering wheel does a small shimmy, as if it's trying to remember something. That's the brake rotor. It got clamped unevenly back in the driveway and it's been wearing out of true ever since, a few ten-thousandths at a time. By the time it shows, the driveway's a month behind him and nobody connects the two. People blame the rotors. The rotors were just standing closest.

## One nut, all the way down

A man who decides to get his life in order tends to pick a nut and run it down.

It's usually a good nut. The body, say. He's up at five, he's lifting, he knows his resting heart rate, he's dropped the twenty pounds and the doctor said "impressive" out loud, which doctors almost never do. Or it's the work: the licence, the promotion, the business that finally turned a profit in its third year. He takes it all the way to spec, and honestly, good for him. It holds. It's the tightest thing on the car.

The rest are finger-tight. The friend he's been meaning to call since spring. The marriage, which is running fine in the sense that the car is running fine. The kid, who's eleven now and has developed opinions. His father, who calls on Sundays and gets about four minutes. He'll get to them. That's the plan, and it's a sincere plan: get this one squared away, then go around.

The trouble is that by "then" the wheel has already seated. The first nut pulled everything toward its side while he was busy with it. The week is built around five a.m. now. Everyone in the house has learned that Saturday mornings are his, and they've arranged themselves around the gap without being asked. Snugging the second nut at this point doesn't bring the wheel flat. It clamps the crooked down harder.

The opposite man exists too, the one with all five nuts finger-tight and the wrench still in its case. This isn't his note. He has troubles of his own, and a lecture on running one down too far isn't among them.

## The expensive part

Here's the part of the manual nobody likes. Sometimes the only way to seat the wheel is to back the first nut off.

Not off. Off a turn. Enough that the wheel can settle back against the hub, so everything can come down together. For a man that might be five a.m. three days a week instead of six, or the business closing the fourth Saturday, and it'll feel like going backwards, because measured on that one nut it is. Nobody gets a trophy for loosening something. The tight nut was real work, and nothing about backing it off says otherwise.

Some nuts don't get loosened for anything. If the five a.m. run is what keeps him sober, that one isn't the problem, and the wheel gets seated around it.

And some seasons don't get a pattern at all. A father in the hospital, a baby who won't sleep, a business with three weeks of cash in it: that isn't a wheel going on in a clean garage. It's a flat on the shoulder in the rain, and on the shoulder a man tightens whatever will get him to the next exit. Nobody sensible grades that. The star is for when there's a garage.

## Fifty miles

Every decent tire shop says the same thing on the way out: come back after fifty miles and we'll check the torque. Wheels settle once they've done some driving. A nut that was right in the bay can be a little under after a week of potholes, and the only way to know is to put the wrench back on and feel which ones move.

A rearranged week settles the same way. The new Saturday holds for a month, and then the old one starts creeping back in, one early alarm at a time. That isn't a character flaw. It's potholes.

Almost nobody goes back. The car feels fine. That's the whole trouble with a wheel: it feels fine right up to the exit ramp.

The shop doesn't chase anybody. The offer just stands, the wrench hangs on the wall, and the check takes about four minutes, which is less time than it takes to find the receipt.
