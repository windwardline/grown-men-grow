# Ghost essay source

The key machine at the hardware store is a simple, honest device. The old key goes in one vise and a blank goes in the other. A stylus rides along the old key's cuts while a spinning wheel carves the same shape into the blank, and a minute and a half later there's a new key and a little drift of brass shavings on the counter.

The machine doesn't know which parts of the old key are the pattern and which parts are wear. It can't. It follows the stylus. Twenty years in a pocket rounds every peak and softens every valley, and the machine copies the rounding just as faithfully as it copies the cuts. Then it adds a little error of its own, because nothing cuts perfect. Copy that copy and the errors stack. Somewhere around the third generation the key goes into the lock and sticks, and a grown man stands on his own porch jiggling it like he's trying to start a lawnmower.

That's why the guy behind the counter, if he's any good, asks whether this is the original. A real locksmith doesn't need the key at all. He cuts from the code: the manufacturer's numbers for how deep each cut was supposed to go. The code has no wear in it. It's what the key was before anybody carried it.

## His father's voice

Most men find out how they were cut at the worst possible moment. His kid sends a full glass of milk across the table, and before he's decided anything, a sentence leaves his mouth in his father's voice. Not the words, which he'd have picked differently. The pitch, the timing, the little sigh in front of it. There's nothing quite like being ventriloquized by a man who's three states away watching the Weather Channel.

That's the machine working. A boy doesn't learn his father from instructions. He learns him from proximity: eighteen years in the vise, the stylus riding every cut. How his father handled a bill he couldn't pay. What his father's face did when somebody cried. Whether apologies happened in that house, or came out sideways as a sudden offer of ice cream. The son copies all of it, and he copies the wear right along with the pattern.

His father was a copy too, which takes some of the heat out of it. He was cut from his own father, in a house with its own weather, by a machine that couldn't tell the difference either. The silence at dinner, the three days of nothing after an argument, the rule that a man handles it himself: most of that was nobody's design. It was wear, carried forward a generation at a time by men who were mostly doing what they could with the key they'd been handed.

## Pattern and wear

Most of what got copied is pattern, though, and it's good. The way his father showed up at six in the morning for a job he didn't love. The way he could fix a dryer with a butter knife and a bad attitude. That he came to the games, or paid for things without making anybody feel it. A man who looks at himself and sees only his father's damage is reading the key as badly as a man who sees none.

The trouble is that wear doesn't announce itself. It feels like personality. "That's just how I am" is what a worn key would say if it could talk. The man who goes quiet for three days after a fight isn't running a strategy. He's running the only cut he was given, and it fits his life about as well as a third-generation key fits a lock.

Some men were cut from a key that never should've been copied. A father who hit, or drank the rent, or left and kept leaving. For those men, none of this is a tidy exercise in sorting good brass from bad, and nobody gets to tell them the old man was doing his best. Maybe he was. It didn't make the house safe. What's true for them is the harder version: the machine copied that too, or they've spent years afraid it did, and checking every cut is heavier work than an essay about keys can carry. It isn't work anybody should have to do in a room by himself, and a lot of men have found somebody to do it with.

Some men had nobody in the other vise at all. They were cut from a description, from what television and coaches and other men's fathers said a man was, and they're still finding out which of those cuts were real.

None of them chose the original. Every one of them gets to look at what he's carrying and ask, cut by cut, which part's the code and which part's twenty years in somebody's pocket. Some men do that with a counselor. Some do it with a brother who grew up in the same house and remembers it completely differently, which is its own education. Some do it on a long drive with the old man himself, the most terrifying option and now and then the best one. Some decide it isn't worth digging into, and that's theirs to decide.

The old key still opens the door. Nobody's asking him to throw it out.

But somebody's being cut from him right now: a kid, a nephew, the apprentice who's already picked up his habit of swearing at the radio. Whether he goes looking at his own cuts is his call. If he does, he'll find there's about half a second between the milk hitting the floor and his father's sentence leaving his mouth. It isn't much. It's where the code lives.
