# Ghost essay source

The driver gets there at 7:15, because the form said 7:15, and the form was written by people who've met drivers. He checks in at a desk that isn't for him. Some places hand him a pager, the round plastic kind a chain restaurant uses to say a table's ready, except this one is going to say his friend is ready, which is not a thing a man in his forties expects to hear from a coaster.

Then he sits. The waiting room has a home renovation show on with the sound off and a basket of magazines old enough to be news again. The coffee is free and has priced itself accordingly. Across the room another man is holding the same pager, and the two of them nod at each other like men who've both been drafted.

Somewhere down the hall, the man he drove in is in a gown that ties in the back, giving his date of birth to a fourth person in a row, and then he isn't giving anybody anything.

## Nothing's recording

What they give a man for a scope, or a wisdom tooth, or a knee that needs a look, doesn't really put him out. It's stranger than that. He stays awake enough to talk, to roll over when asked, to tell whoever's nearest that they have great shoes. What the drug switches off is the recording. The camera's running and there's no tape in it.

So the clinic has a rule. It's printed on the form, it's repeated on the phone the day before, and it doesn't bend. He doesn't leave alone. Not in a cab, not in a rideshare, not on foot, and not "my truck's right outside, I'll just sit in it a while." Somebody's name goes on the line marked responsible adult, and that somebody is in the building when he comes out, or he doesn't come out. It's the one line on the form a man can't fill in by himself.

The rule isn't kindness. It's a liability policy, written by a lawyer who's met patients. The kindness is whoever's name is on the line.

## The instructions go to the driver

The pager goes off around ten. A nurse walks the driver back to a curtained bay, where his friend is sitting up in a recliner, half dressed, holding a cup of apple juice with tremendous seriousness.

Then the nurse turns to the driver and gives him the instructions.

Not the patient. The driver. The patient is right there, nodding, agreeing with everything, and the nurse talks past him with the ease of years, because the nurse knows what he doesn't, which is that none of this is staying. Nothing to drink tonight. Nothing heavy to lift. No driving until tomorrow. No big decisions and nothing legally binding signed for twenty-four hours, which makes it the one afternoon of his adult life when a professional has advised him not to sign anything.

The driver gets a folder. The driver gets told which symptoms mean call this number and which mean go straight to the emergency room. The driver gets asked whether he has any questions, and the man in the recliner says, "I'm good," which nobody writes down.

The ride home is about twenty-five minutes, and the driver spends them answering. How long was he in there? About an hour. Did the parking cost anything? Eight bucks, don't worry about it. Was that apple juice? Because that was the best apple juice anybody's ever had. It was apple juice. How long was he in there?

The answer doesn't get better with practice, and it doesn't have to. Nothing's recording on the passenger side, so the drive has exactly one person who'll remember it, and he's driving.

There's apology in it too, because some part of a man knows, even through the fog, that he's being hauled. "You didn't have to wait." The clinic says somebody did. "I could've gotten an Uber." The clinic says he couldn't. "I owe you one." That one goes unanswered, partly because the driver is merging, and partly because it isn't a debt anybody's going to collect.

The driver doesn't get all of it right, either. He takes the highway when the back way's faster. He parked on the wrong level this morning and spent ten minutes hunting for his own car. And somewhere around the second exit he unwraps the breakfast sandwich he bought from the cart in the lobby, in a closed car, beside a man who hasn't eaten since yesterday at noon and has opinions about that, which he also won't keep.

There's a man who leaves the line on the form blank because no name comes to mind, and his appointment slides to next spring, and then to the spring after. The rule doesn't bend for him either.

At the house, the driver gets him in the door and onto the couch, puts the folder on the kitchen counter, and finds the remote, which in most houses is the hardest part of the day. A glass of water goes where it can be reached. The driver says he'll call later, and does, and the call goes to voicemail, because the man on the couch is asleep.

He'll remember being wheeled to the curb, roughly. He won't remember the drive. In the morning the folder is on the counter where it was left, and across the top of the discharge sheet, in somebody else's handwriting, is the time he's allowed to drive again.
