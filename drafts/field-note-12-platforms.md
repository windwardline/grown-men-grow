# LinkedIn Page

Every lifting sling carries two numbers, and the one printed on the tag is the smaller by a factor of about five. The tag gives the working load limit. The other number, the breaking strength, is where the thing actually comes apart.

The engineer who wrote down the smaller number was not losing his nerve. He was pricing in the day the load swings on the way up, the day the legs are at a worse angle than anyone measured, the day somebody hooks it in a hurry because the truck is idling. A capacity that only holds when conditions are perfect is not a capacity.

Men understand this completely at work and almost not at all about themselves. Asked what he can handle, a man answers with his breaking strength — and then books the rest of his life at that figure.

The new field note is about what happens next, and about the part most of us get wrong: under load, he does not get to choose what fails. What parts first is whatever has the least tension on it — no invoice, no deadline, nobody who will make a scene. Which is why the work almost never fails. [canonical link]

# Substack Notes

## Note 1

A sling rated at two tons does not fail at two tons. It fails somewhere well past ten. The gap between those two numbers is the whole discipline, and it exists because loads swing and angles are worse than anyone measured.

## Note 2

The overload is rarely one heroic yes. It is the extra account, the coaching nobody else stepped up for, the kitchen a contractor would take four months to do. Every one of those is defensible on its own, which is exactly how the total gets past him.

## Note 3 — canonical link

Under load a man does not get to choose what fails. The work has a client and a deadline and a number attached, so the work gets the good hours. [canonical link]
