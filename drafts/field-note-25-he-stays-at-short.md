# Ghost essay source

A routine ground ball to shortstop is about as close to a sure thing as amateur baseball offers. Two hops, glove down, throw to first. Men who can't remember their own anniversaries have made that play a thousand times without thinking about it, which is the trouble, because the thousand-and-first is the one where the ball stays low and he doesn't.

It goes under the glove. It rolls into left. The runner who should be jogging back to his dugout is standing on first base, trying very hard not to look delighted.

The scorebook has a mark for this. Every position on the field has a number, and shortstop is six, so the record is efficient about it: E6. Two characters. Anybody in the park who can read a scorebook knows exactly who, and in a men's league the official scorer is usually whoever remembered to bring a pencil.

## Four outs

The part that matters isn't the E.

An inning is three outs, and he's just handed the other team a fourth. The pitcher did his job. He got the ground ball, the exact ball he was trying to get, and now he has to go and get another one. Every coach who ever lived has a sentence about this, and it's always the same sentence: you can't give a good team four outs.

The rulebook handles it in a way that's stranger and kinder than it needs to be. When the scorer works out which runs count against the pitcher, he replays the inning in his head as though the ball had been caught. Once that imaginary inning reaches three outs, it's over. Anything that scores after that point still goes on the scoreboard, and the other team gets to keep it, but it's marked unearned. That's the scorebook's way of saying the runs belong to somebody who isn't the man throwing.

So for the rest of that inning the pitcher's working an inning that, on paper, already ended. Everybody on the field is. They're playing on past the end for a mistake none of them made.

## Nobody comes out of the dugout

That's the whole event, and from the bleachers it doesn't look like anything.

The manager could take him out. It would cost almost nothing to slide him over to second and put somebody steadier at short. He doesn't. He stands on the top step with his arms folded, chewing on something, and leaves him there.

The pitcher gets the ball back, takes a slow walk around the mound, and goes to work on the next hitter. Strike one. Strike two. He's doing the most generous thing one man can do for another on a ball field, which is to get on with it.

None of it's clean. The pitcher kicks the rubber first, hard enough that the whole infield hears it, and keeps his back turned a second longer than the rosin bag strictly requires. The second baseman jogs over and says "you're fine" so fast it comes out as one word, and both of them know he isn't; he just booted a two-hopper in front of everybody's kids. Somebody in the dugout yells "wake up, six," which is meant as a joke and gets about forty percent of the way there. And in the parking lot afterward there's a bit about it. The bit runs one beer longer than it should, and it'll be back next season, probably by name.

He doesn't take any of it well. He apologises to the pitcher between pitches, which is the one thing the pitcher has no time for right then. He says "my fault" to the second baseman, who knew. At the end of the inning he tells the manager he can sit if they want, and the manager tells him to go get a drink of water and writes nothing down.

He isn't being kept on because he earned it. He's just cost them, conspicuously, at the one position where everybody can see. He's being kept on anyway.

This happens in rooms with no infield. A man prices a job wrong and the company eats it, and next week his name's still on the schedule. A man misses something that was his to catch, and two other men stay until seven doing it again and don't mention it to the super where he can hear. A man says a thing at a family table that he'd give a great deal to have back, and the next holiday he's still invited, still handed the carving knife, and nobody brings it up but his brother, once, in the driveway, badly.

In every one of them somebody else gets the fourth out. He doesn't, and he can't. That's what makes it an error rather than a slump. The play's over, and whatever happens next is done by other people, in an inning that should already have ended.

Not every failure gets scored as an error, and it would be a lie to pretend otherwise. An error is a misplay on a ball that ordinary effort turns into an out. Some of what a man does isn't that. Some of it gets a man walked off the field in the middle of an inning, and it should, and that's the longest walk in the game. This isn't about those. It's about the ordinary ball he's handled a thousand times, and the people who keep him on the field after the one he didn't.

## The ball finds him

Every man who's ever played believes the ball finds the guy who just made an error. Coaches believe it. Children believe it within two seasons. It has never once been a comfort to anybody.

It does find him, more often than seems fair. Two batters later: same two hops, same patch of dirt. The throw goes across to first like something being mailed that's marked fragile.

It doesn't change the book. Nothing that happens in the fourth or the seventh reaches back into the third and rubs out a letter. The pitcher's line will show the runs, and beside them a smaller number that leaves them out. The E stays under the shortstop's name, in pencil, for good.

Next week's lineup card has him at short.
