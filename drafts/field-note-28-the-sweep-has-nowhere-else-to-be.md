# Ghost essay source

The gap opens the way gaps always open, which is politely. Nobody attacks. The group just goes up the hill at the speed it went along the flat, and somewhere around the second switchback the wheel in front of him is ten feet away, then thirty, then registered in a different county.

From the back, getting dropped feels like a conversation moving into the next room. The jerseys get smaller. The talk goes first, then the clicking of gears, and then there's nothing left but his own breathing, which on a hill like this isn't a sound a man would choose to be alone with.

He isn't alone with it. The sweep is behind him.

Every no-drop ride has a sweep, or says it does. The ride leader sits at the front and knows the route. The sweep sits at the back and knows the route too, plus one more thing, which is where the last man is at all times, because the last man is the job. The sweep carries the spare tube, the pump, the tire levers, the multitool, and usually a second tube for whoever didn't bring the first. The rule fits on the flyer: nobody rides behind the sweep. Anybody behind the sweep isn't on the ride anymore. He's just a man on a bicycle, out for some air.

So the sweep's place is fixed to the slowest rider, not to the road. If the last man is going nine miles an hour up the hill, so is the sweep, who could do eighteen, and who has to do the nine in a gear so easy the pedals barely count. It looks like a sheepdog walking one elderly sheep to the barn.

Some Saturdays it's a knee. Some years it's the year: the divorce, the job that ate his evenings, the winter that put ten pounds on him and took the top off his lungs. The sweep doesn't ask which. Back there it all rides at nine miles an hour.

## Go on ahead

The sweep hears "go on, I'm fine" about once a ride. It gets said between breaths, so it arrives as two separate offers. There's an answer for it, and the answer is that going on ahead isn't something the job contains. A sweep who goes on ahead isn't a sweep. He's a man who left somebody on a hill and went for coffee.

So the sweep stays. He drifts up alongside on the steep bits and makes conversation, which is a mixed blessing, because the sweep has all the breath in the world and the man he's sweeping has none, so the conversation runs about eighty percent one way. He tells him it flattens out after the bend. It doesn't flatten out after the bend. It hasn't flattened out after that bend since the glaciers left. The sweep says it every Saturday to whoever's back there, and it's wrong every Saturday, and it still gets the last man to the bend.

Half the time the sweep is the strongest rider in the club, and volunteered because the front bores him. That doesn't make him a saint. It makes him a man who'd rather talk than race, which is its own kind of selfish, and the last man gets carried by it anyway.

On the flat the sweep does the one useful thing a strong rider can do for a tired one. He slides in front and gives him a wheel to sit on. Riding in another man's draft takes a real share of the work off at speed, more into a headwind, and the sweep takes the wind full in the face so the man behind him doesn't have to. Nobody mentions it. The sweep just turns up in front, at exactly the speed that's possible, and stays there until the next turn, where he drops back again to make sure the last man made it round.

The group waits at the top of the climb. That's the regroup, and it's real, and it has a flaw that everyone who's ever been at the back knows by heart. The group gets there first and rests. They drink, they eat half a bar, somebody fiddles with a cleat. Five minutes. Then the last man crests the hill with the sweep at his shoulder, and the group, rested and perfectly polite, clips in and rolls out. The one rider who needed the stop gets about forty seconds of it. The sweep buys him another minute by asking about his tire pressure, a subject neither of them has ever cared about.

He apologises at the top, naturally. He apologises at the bottom, and at the light, and into the sweep's back on the flat, where the wind takes it before it lands. The sweep accepts every one of them the way a bartender accepts a coaster.

None of this makes him faster. The sweep isn't a training plan, and nobody back there is promising the man a better Saturday next month. Not every ride has one, either. Plenty of rides advertised as no-drop turn out to mean no-drop for the first ten miles, and a man finds that out alone on a county road, reading the route off his phone at a stop sign. That's a long morning, and it's a common one.

On this ride the sweep has nowhere else to be. He rolls into the coffee shop last, right behind the man he came in with, and leans his bike against the window.

The sweep's ride uploads like everyone else's. It's the slowest ride of his week by a distance, an average speed that wouldn't trouble a beach cruiser, and it sits on his profile under his name for anybody to see, with no caption.
